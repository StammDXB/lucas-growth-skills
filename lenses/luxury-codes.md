# Lens: Luxury Codes

Load when: the brand is luxury, premium, or aspirational, or when the work
involves scarcity, provenance, pricing power, or exclusivity.

Luxury inverts several rules that hold everywhere else. Applying performance
marketing logic to a luxury brand is not merely suboptimal, it is corrosive, and
the damage is slow and hard to reverse.

## The central mechanism: desirability decays with availability

**Cardinization.** Pierre Cardin licensed his name from 280 licensees in 34
countries (1973) to roughly 800 to 900 licenses across 100 to 140 countries,
reaching about $1bn in annual wholesale by 1987. The name appeared on radiators,
bicycle pumps, cigarettes, hair dryers, and car interiors, over 2,000 products by
the late 1970s. Eric Briones, Paris School of Luxury: "Ubiquity has killed the
desirability of the brand. With this infinite multiplication of licenses, it is
the value-quality that has suffered." The pattern is now taught as
"cardinization."
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Cardin_(brand)

**Halston.** Signed a $1bn JCPenney deal in 1983 aiming to "dress every woman in
America." Bergdorf Goodman, a two-decade partner, dropped his main line
immediately on announcement. He had already sold his trademark in 1973 and by
1984 had lost control of his own name.
Source: https://www.bustle.com/entertainment/how-halston-lost-his-company-name-true-story

**Burberry.** Between 2001 and 2005 the check became attached to "chav" and
football hooligan culture, accelerated by counterfeiting and by affordable
entry-price accessories, notably check baseball caps, which the company
eventually ordered discontinued and cleared from shelves. The recovery cut check
usage to roughly 10% of product, brought fragrance and beauty licenses in-house,
and bought out the Spanish franchise (about 20% of group revenue).
Sources: https://www.highsnobiety.com/p/burberrys-nova-check-print/ and
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burberry

**Applied:** entry-price products are the usual vector. They recruit a volume
audience faster than the brand can absorb it, and the signature asset becomes
the thing that signals the wrong group. Ask what the cheapest item carrying the
brand's most recognisable code is.

## Discounting is a positioning decision, not a pricing decision

Michael Kors CEO John Idol, Q1 FY2017 earnings call, on why the company pulled
out of department store discounting: it was "creating confusion in the
consumers' mind relative to the value of the Michael Kors brand when it's being
seen so often on sale in so many different places," and the company had to
"correct something that we think is actually having a negative long-term effect
for the brand." The pullback was estimated to cost over $150m in annual revenue.
Source: https://www.retaildive.com/news/michael-kors-calls-halt-to-department-store-discounts-citing-brand-damage/424277/

Coach at its early-2010s peak generated north of 60% of profits from outlet
stores and lost more than half its market cap between 2012 and 2014.
Source: https://artofthebrand.substack.com/p/how-coach-became-retails-biggest

**Applied:** a discount is a public statement about what the thing is worth. In
luxury, willingness to discount is read as evidence the price was never real.

## Pricing power is the strategy, not a lever

Chanel Medium Classic Flap, US retail: $4,900 (2015-16), $5,800 (2019), $6,500
(2020), $7,800 (2021), $8,800 (2022), $10,200 (2023), $10,800 (2024), $11,300
(2025). Roughly 139% over a period of about 40% US inflation.
Sources: https://luxuryevermore.com/blogs/article/chanel-classic-flap-bag-price-history
and https://www.pursebop.com/breaking-news-chanel-price-increase-2025/

Hermes Birkin: Baghunter's study of consignment-sourced, blind-stamp-dated
receipts 1980 to 2015 found resale values "have never fluctuated downwards,"
averaging 14.2% annual increase.
Source: https://baghunter.com/pages/hermes-birkin-values-research-study

**The formal mechanism:** Bagwell and Bernheim, "Veblen Effects in a Theory of
Conspicuous Consumption," showing conspicuous-consumption demand can rationally
produce positive price effects when price signals quality.
Source: https://www.jstor.org/stable/2118201

**Applied:** rising price is itself a message. Holding price flat in luxury can
read as weakening demand. But see the limit below.

## The limits, which matter in 2026

**There is a ceiling, and the industry hit it.** Luca Solca, Bernstein, November
2025: "When you look at production costs versus retail prices... the markup has
reached ten, fifteen times or more. At that point you touch unsustainable
extremes."
Source: https://www.nssmag.com/en/fashion/43483/uxury-crisis-2025-2026-luca-solca-bernstein-interview

**The customer base is contracting.** Bain and Altagamma: the personal luxury
customer base fell from about 400 million (2022) to roughly 340 million (2025),
the first contraction in the study's 23-year history. Active shoppers fell from
about 60% of the addressable base to 40-45%. The top 2% of customers now
generate 45% of purchases, yet "feel less pampered by brands, as their
experiences have become more transactional."
Sources: https://www.bain.com/insights/luxury-in-transition-securing-future-growth/
and https://www.bain.com/about/media-center/press-releases/20252/global-luxury-stays-resilient-despite-economic-headwinds-and-shifting-consumer-trends-that-reshape-marketbain--company-and-altagamma/

**Quiet luxury.** Han et al. (2010) found status-secure wealthy individuals
prefer inconspicuous goods with subtle branding over logo-forward pieces. Loud
signalling skews toward the status-insecure.
Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiet_luxury

## The counter-insight that stops this becoming dogma

Overexposure is not a universal explanation. Solca on Gucci: "Gucci works when
it's over-the-top... When the brand tried to do something different, the results
were disastrous." Gucci's 2023-2025 slump followed moving **away** from
logo-forward maximalism, not saturation of it.

**Applied:** diagnose before prescribing restraint. Some brands are built on
loudness, and quieting them destroys the thing people bought. Ask what the brand
actually promises before recommending subtlety.

## Honest gaps

Do not claim these. They were searched for and not found.

- **No luxury-specific evidence for the 60/40 brand-to-activation split.** Binet
  and Field's work draws on IPA Databank cases weighted toward large established
  B2C brands, and no publication applying it to luxury could be located. Do not
  cite 60/40 as a luxury rule.
- **No verifiable Bain or McKinsey study specifically on discounting and luxury
  brand equity.** The Michael Kors and Coach cases above are real, but they are
  cases, not a controlled finding.
- **Performance marketing is not categorically incompatible with luxury.** A
  dedicated luxury performance-marketing services market exists: Interluxe Group
  acquired adMixt in 2026, serving Rolls-Royce, Four Seasons, Range Rover, and
  Ferragamo. Treat the tension as real but contested, not settled.
  Source: https://www.modernretail.co/marketing/interluxe-group-acquires-admixt-in-bid-to-marry-events-with-performance-marketing-for-luxury-brands/

## Benchmarks worth knowing

LVMH FY2024: advertising and promotion was 11.5% of revenue; total marketing and
selling expenses €31,002m on €84,683m revenue (36.6%). Richemont FY2025:
communication expenses €2,093m on €21,399m sales (9.8%). Kering discloses no
distinct marketing line item.
Sources: LVMH FY2024 financial documents; Richemont FY25 annual report.
