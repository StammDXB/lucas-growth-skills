# Lens: Consumer Psychology

Load when: reasoning about why someone buys, choosing a persuasion mechanism,
structuring a price or a set of options, or being asked to apply a named
behavioural effect.

Behavioural science is the most confidently mis-cited body of work in marketing.
Much of what circulates as settled was overturned, never replicated, or holds
only in hypothetical lab choices and collapses with real money. This lens grades
what you are about to lean on.

**Use it with `evidence-and-proof`.** That lens governs numbers. This one governs
mechanisms. The failure modes are cousins: quoting an effect size you cannot
source is the same error as quoting a benchmark you cannot source.

## Grade the effect before you use it

**Well supported. Use, and name the mechanism.**

- **Anchoring on numeric judgement.** Among the more reliably replicated effects
  in the literature; it survived the Many Labs multi-site replication (Klein et
  al., 2014). Strongest where the person has no firm prior. Weakens as expertise
  and price salience rise.
- **Left-digit effect (charm pricing).** £9.99 is encoded closer to £9 than £10
  because encoding begins before the digits are all read (Thomas & Morwitz,
  2005). Real, and specifically about the leftmost digit changing — not about
  the number ending in 9. £4.99 to £5.49 gains nothing.
- **Specificity of a descriptive norm.** Goldstein, Cialdini & Griskevicius
  (2008): the more the reference group resembles the person, the higher the
  compliance. See `evidence-and-proof` for the figures and the caution against
  extrapolating them.
- **Defaults.** Among the most robust interventions in applied behavioural work.
  What is preselected is what happens. Note the ethical line below.

**Contested. Usable only with the caveat stated out loud.**

- **Loss aversion.** The 2:1 "losses loom twice as large" ratio is far shakier
  than its ubiquity suggests; Gal & Rucker (2018) argue the general claim is not
  well supported. Loss framing sometimes beats gain framing and sometimes does
  not. Never promise a multiplier.
- **The decoy effect (asymmetric dominance).** Reliable in hypothetical choices
  between abstract options; fragile or absent with real products and real money
  (Frederick et al., 2014; Yang & Lynn, 2014). A decoy tier is a legitimate
  design, but do not present it as a reliable lever.
- **Scarcity and urgency.** The principle is real; the field evidence is mixed
  and effects are strongly moderated by whether the constraint is believed.
  Manufactured scarcity that the buyer detects reverses the effect and costs
  trust. See `offer-design` for the hard gate on fabricated urgency.

**Do not use. These failed replication.**

- **Ego depletion.** Willpower as a depleting resource. A registered multi-lab
  replication found essentially no effect (Hagger et al., 2016).
- **Social/behavioural priming.** The famous elderly-words-slow-your-walk result
  did not replicate under blinded conditions (Doyen et al., 2012). Treat the
  whole family of "subtle cue changes downstream behaviour" claims as unsafe.
- **Any "N% lift from applying [bias]" figure.** No such number is transportable
  between contexts. Cite the mechanism, never a multiplier.

## The decision models worth keeping

Plain reasoning already covers most thinking-tool taxonomies, so only three earn
space here — the ones marketers most often skip.

**Theory of constraints.** Every funnel has exactly one binding constraint.
Optimising anywhere else produces no throughput. This is the single most
frequently violated idea in marketing execution: rewriting a CTA on a page whose
constraint is traffic intent changes nothing, and it is the reason
`experiment-design` gates on locating the constraint before producing variants.

**Second-order effects.** Ask what the effect of the effect is. A discount lifts
this quarter's revenue and teaches the customer to wait for the next discount.
Most marketing damage is second-order and shows up after the campaign is judged.

**Local versus global optima.** Subject-line testing cannot rescue a channel that
was the wrong channel. Zoom out before zooming in.

## The ethical line

The mechanisms above work on real people, and the strong ones work whether or not
the underlying offer is good.

The test: **would this still work if the person could see exactly what you were
doing?** A default that saves someone a step survives that test. A pre-ticked
insurance box does not. Scarcity that is true survives it. A countdown that
resets on refresh does not.

Apply the mechanism to make a true thing legible and easy to act on. The moment
it is compensating for a proposition that cannot stand on its own, the work
belongs in `offer-design`, not in persuasion technique.
