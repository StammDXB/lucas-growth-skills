# Lens: Evidence and Proof

Load when: making any claim about what works in marketing, or citing a
framework, study, or statistic.

Marketing is unusually full of confidently repeated numbers with no traceable
origin. The default failure mode when generating marketing content is emitting
folklore in an authoritative voice. This lens exists to prevent that.

## The grading discipline

Tag every substantive claim internally before using it:

- **VERIFIED** — you have read the primary source
- **SECONDARY** — a credible source cites a primary you have not read
- **FOLKLORE** — widely repeated, traceable to nothing

Never present FOLKLORE as fact. If a claim matters to the argument and you
cannot grade it above folklore, either drop it or state the uncertainty. Saying
"this is widely repeated but I could not trace it to a study" costs nothing and
is the difference between advice and noise.

**Corollary:** when a user asks you to apply a folklore rule, correct the premise
and still do the job. Do not silently comply, and do not refuse the task.

## The effectiveness finding most often inverted

Peter Field's analysis found that awarded campaigns "are now no more effective
than non-awarded campaigns." This is real and confirmed on the IPA's own
material.

**The misreading:** "creative quality does not predict effectiveness."

**What the precursor work actually shows:** awarded work got starved of budget
and pushed onto short-term evaluation windows. The cause is a change in how
creative work is funded and measured, not evidence that creativity stopped
working.

A skill that concludes creativity is irrelevant has inverted the finding. Cite it
carefully or not at all.

## Claims to refuse

These were specifically checked and failed verification. Do not present them as
sourced.

**The 60/40 brand-to-activation split.** Real and IPA-sourced, revised to 62/38.
But it derives from self-selected IPA *award submissions*, not a representative
sample, and Sharp's methodological critique of that sampling is confirmed. Report
both halves or neither. There is no luxury-specific version of this finding.

**The middle rungs of the Cannes Creative Effectiveness Ladder.** Only
"influential idea" (lowest) and "enduring icon" (highest) are confirmed. A
purported full list circulates but was flagged at source as AI-generated.

**"Unique mechanism."** Widely used in direct-response circles. Traces to no
identifiable originator and no peer-reviewed source. Folk terminology, not a
framework. Use the concept if it helps, but do not attribute it.

**The ESOV coefficient.** Two conflicting secondary figures circulate (roughly
0.5 versus 0.7 points of share growth per 10 points of excess share of voice).
Unresolvable without the primary, which would not render.

**Ogilvy's five big-idea questions.** Convergent across secondary sources but the
primary text was unreachable. Paraphrase, never quote.

**"80% read the headline, 20% read the rest."** No measurement traces to it. A
mutation of Ogilvy's "five times as many" line, which Ogilvy himself supported
with no study.

**Spam trigger words.** Not the mechanism behind modern email filtering.

**Any conversion lift percentage without a named study.** Most circulating CRO
statistics have no traceable primary source.

## A tension to hold rather than resolve

Reeves' **Unique Selling Proposition** and Sharp's **distinctiveness** thesis
directly contradict each other. Sharp: "throw that Unique Selling Proposition out
the door. Because customers don't care."

Both are well evidenced. A skill should hold both and know which context each
governs, rather than picking a winner. Broadly: USP logic fits considered,
differentiated, high-involvement purchases. Distinctiveness logic fits
low-involvement, habitual, high-penetration categories. State which you are
applying and why.

## What counts as proof in a deliverable

When the work itself needs to be credible, the hierarchy is:

1. Demonstration. Showing the thing working.
2. Specific quantified results with a named source.
3. Named identifiable people, with role and photo.
4. Independent third-party validation.
5. Guarantees and risk reversal. These reduce anxiety rather than prove anything.
6. Unattributed testimonials. Near zero value, and actively suspicious.
7. Vanity counts and logo bars.

**The specificity effect has real experimental backing.** Goldstein, Cialdini and
Griskevicius (2008), *Journal of Consumer Research* 35(3): a hotel towel-reuse
field experiment where a generic descriptive norm produced 44.1% compliance
against 35.1% for a standard appeal, and the most specific norm (guests "who
stayed in this room") produced 49.3% against a 37.2% baseline.

Narrowing a claim from general to specifically-like-you measurably increased
compliance. Cite the mechanism. Do not extrapolate it into a testimonial lift
percentage.

## No framework theatre

**Never name a methodology you are not actually applying.**

Dropping "we used jobs-to-be-done" or "this follows the value proposition canvas"
without doing the work is framework theatre. It borrows the authority of a
framework while skipping the thinking that gives it value, and it is very hard
for a non-expert client to detect.

The test: if you name a framework, you must be able to produce its actual
components filled in. If you cannot, describe what you did in plain language
instead. Plain reasoning honestly labelled beats a named framework applied in
name only.

The same applies to citing an expert. Naming Schwartz, Cialdini, or Sharp does
not make an argument stronger unless you are applying the specific thing they
established, and can say which thing.

## The rule that matters most

**Never invent a statistic, customer name, testimonial, result, or award.**

If the user has not supplied proof, say so and weaken the claim to something
true. An honest small claim outperforms an unbelievable large one, and a
fabricated one is a liability that outlives the campaign.
