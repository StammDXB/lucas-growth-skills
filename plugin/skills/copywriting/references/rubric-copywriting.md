# Rubric: Copywriting

Score the draft against every line. PASS or FAIL, no partial credit. Quote
evidence from the draft for each verdict. The burden of proof is on PASS: if you
cannot cite the evidence, it is a FAIL.

Score what you wrote, not what you intended to write.

## A. Diagnosis

**A1. The reader is specific.**
PASS requires you to name the reader in one sentence, with a role, context, and
current belief. "Marketers" fails. "Ops leads who already use a TMS and think
switching costs too much" passes.

**A2. The copy starts at the reader's actual awareness stage.**
Quote the opening. State the stage you targeted. PASS only if the opening
matches. Explaining a known problem to a product-aware reader is a FAIL. So is
naming the product in line one to an unaware reader.

**A3. The claim is calibrated to market sophistication.**
In a market that has heard the claim before, PASS requires a new mechanism,
frame, or reader identity. A louder version of the incumbent claim is a FAIL.

**A4. One action.**
There is exactly one thing you want the reader to do, and it is unambiguous.
Two competing calls to action is a FAIL.

## B. Substance

**B1. Every claim has proof adjacent to it.**
List each claim and the proof beside it. A claim with no proof within the same
section is a FAIL, unless it was deliberately weakened to an honest statement.

**B2. No invented facts.**
Zero fabricated statistics, customer names, testimonials, results, or awards.
Any number must trace to something the user supplied. A single invented fact
fails the whole draft, not just this line.

**B3. Specificity over abstraction.**
Find the three most important sentences. Each must contain something concrete: a
number, a named thing, a time, a mechanism. If all three are abstract, FAIL.

**B4. Objections are addressed.**
Name the top two objections this reader would raise. PASS requires the copy to
handle both, at the point where the reader would raise them, not in a FAQ
afterthought.

## C. Craft

**C1. Sentence rhythm varies.**
Count the words in each sentence of the longest paragraph. If the spread is
narrow and the lengths cluster, FAIL. Uniform rhythm is the strongest tell of
machine-written copy.

**C2. No inflated verbs standing in for claims.**
Scan for: revolutionize, transform, unlock, supercharge, elevate, empower,
seamless, robust, cutting-edge, game-changing. Each instance must be justified
or cut. Unjustified instances are a FAIL.

**C3. No hedging.**
Scan for: can help, may improve, potentially, designed to, aims to. Each either
commits or gets cut. Hedged claims are a FAIL.

**C4. The opening earns the second line.**
Read only the first sentence. If it is scaffolding, context-setting, or could
open any piece in the category, FAIL.

**C5. Written to one person.**
Second person singular throughout. "Companies like yours" and "businesses today"
are a FAIL.

## D. Distinctiveness

**D1. It could not be any competitor's copy.**
Substitute a competitor's name into the draft. If it still reads as true and
natural, FAIL. This is the commutation test applied to copy.

**D2. Voice is held consistently.**
If a brand voice was supplied, every section matches it. If none was supplied,
the draft still has one consistent voice rather than drifting register.

## E. Integrity

**E1. Urgency and scarcity are real.**
Any deadline, limit, or scarcity claim must be factually true. Manufactured
urgency is a FAIL regardless of performance.

**E2. Nothing manipulative.**
No shame, no false stakes, no fake social proof, no confirm-shaming in the
opt-out. FAIL on any instance.

**E3. Assumptions are disclosed.**
Anything you assumed rather than were told appears in the delivery note. Silent
assumptions are a FAIL.

## Scoring

Count FAILs. Fix all of them, then re-score. Maximum two rounds.

If a line still fails after two rounds, it is a real constraint rather than a
writing problem. Report it in the delivery note in plain language. Do not
silently drop it, and do not keep looping.
