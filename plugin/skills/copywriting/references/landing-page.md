# Landing, Sales, and Product Pages

Load when: writing or rewriting a landing page, sales page, product page, or
homepage hero.

**Evidence warning.** The copywriting canon is largely untested craft wisdom and
the CRO literature is largely untraceable case studies. Three bodies of evidence
here are genuinely solid: Nielsen Norman Group's eyetracking, the
Goldstein/Cialdini field experiment, and Evan Miller's testing math. Everything
else is convention. The do-not-cite blocklist at the end is as important as the
positive content.

## Route on awareness and sophistication

Awareness sets **where the page starts**. Sophistication sets **what argument
can still land**. Most copy advice handles the first and ignores the second.

**Awareness** (Schwartz): unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware, product-aware,
most-aware.

**Sophistication**, the neglected half:

1. **First to market.** State the claim plainly. It has never been heard.
2. **Claim escalates.** Competitors copied it, so enlarge it.
3. **New mechanism.** The claim is exhausted. Shift to *how it works*.
4. **Mechanism escalates.** Rivals copied the mechanism. Claim a better one.
5. **Identification.** Claims and mechanisms are burned out. Sell who the reader
   is, not what the product does.

A stage-5 market given stage-1 copy reads as naive. A stage-1 market given
stage-5 identity copy reads as vague.

Source note: Schwartz's *Breakthrough Advertising* (1966) is paywalled on every
archive route. The stage names above are field-consensus reconstruction, and the
framework's live professional use is verifiable (Copyhackers operates on it
explicitly), but they are not primary-text confirmed. Say so if asked.

### When the classic frameworks break

- **AIDA breaks on warm traffic.** It builds from zero attention. On retargeting
  or list traffic, the attention-grabbing hook burns trust already paid for.
- **PAS breaks at both ends.** Agitating a problem an unaware reader does not
  know they have reads as manipulation. Agitating a most-aware reader's solved
  problem reads as insulting.
- **Sugarman's slippery slide assumes a linear reader.** NN/g measured 79% of
  users scanning rather than reading. The metaphor fits VSL scripts and email,
  not scanned mobile pages.
- **Caples' rules came from keyed-coupon mail order.** They generalise only where
  response is genuinely trackable per variant.
- **Hopkins' reason-why needs an actual reason.** On parity products the doctrine
  has nothing to operate on.

## Traffic temperature

| | Cold paid | Warm email | Branded / high intent |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reader state | Unaware to problem-aware | Solution to product-aware | Product to most-aware |
| Headline job | Name the problem or the enemy | Extend the known promise | Confirm they are in the right place |
| Offer appears | After pain and mechanism | Early-middle | Immediately |
| Length | Longest | Medium | Shortest |
| Dominant risk | Under-explaining | Repeating what they know | Friction before the ask |

**Message match.** Carry the ad's actual phrasing into the headline. Oli
Gardner: "If your ad copy doesn't match the message on your landing page, you're
disrespecting the click."

## Where attention actually goes

NN/g 2018 eyetracking, 120 participants, 130,000+ fixations:

- **57% of viewing time above the fold**, down from 80% in NN/g's 2010 data
- **74% within two screenfuls, 81% within three**
- **Over 42% of total page time in the top fifth of the page**
- Users have time to read at most **~28% of words** on an average visit

Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/scrolling-and-attention/

**Encode this, not the folk version.** The fold is not a wall, it is the front
of a steep decay curve. People scroll routinely. But ~80% of attention lands in
the first three screenfuls, so the value proposition, primary proof, and first
CTA belong there. Below screen three you are writing for a self-selected
minority: correct for deep proof, FAQ, and objection handling; wrong for the
value proposition.

## Page anatomy

Long-form sales page sequence, each section with a job:

1. **Trust opener, before any claim.** Removes the scepticism barrier.
2. **Pain in voice-of-customer language.** Produces "that is so me."
3. **Agitation, including why previous solutions failed.** Names a common enemy.
4. **Solution introduction, built to answer the number one objection** before
   proceeding.
5. **Product detail and proof of feasibility.** Only after trust and pain land.
6. **Price**, after value is built.
7. **Close:** CTA plus risk reversal plus testimonial, stacked.

Short lead-gen page: benefit headline matched to the referring ad, copy
answering "does this have what I need / can I trust this / how long will this
take," **one possible action**, no navigation, form repeated on scroll.

**Unsettled, and treat as judgment rather than rule:** whether the headline
leads with pain or with benefit. Wiebe and Cho openly disagree and do not
resolve it.

## The headline

**Be honest about the evidence here, because this is where the field is worst.**

The "8 out of 10 read the headline, 2 out of 10 read the rest" claim is
**FOLKLORE**. No measurement traces to it. It is a mutation of Ogilvy's line into
rounder numbers, and the 80/20 framing does not even follow arithmetically from
"five times as many."

Ogilvy's actual line, "on the average, five times as many people read the
headline as read the body copy," is unverified as to book and page, and
**Ogilvy cited no study**. It was asserted professional observation.

The real measured evidence comes from Caples, who ran keyed-coupon mail-order
ads where response was attributable per variant. His consistent finding:
self-interest and news headlines reliably outpull clever or cute ones.

Better teaching artifact than any statistic: Ogilvy's Rolls-Royce headline, "At
60 miles an hour the loudest noise in this new Rolls-Royce comes from the
electric clock." A specific, checkable, sensory proof claim used as a headline.

**Patterns worth using** (craft convention, not measured):
- Quantified delta: "{Metric} from {bad number} to {good number}"
- Outcome without the feared cost: "{Outcome} without {pain}"
- Category for audience: "The {category} for {specific audience}"
- News: "{New mechanism} means {old constraint} no longer applies"
- Sensory proof as headline: one checkable concrete fact implying the whole claim

Tests: could a competitor use it unchanged? Does it contain something checkable?
Does it echo the traffic source's words?

## Proof

**The strongest evidence in this whole reference is about specificity, and it is
a real field experiment.**

Goldstein, Cialdini and Griskevicius (2008), "A Room with a Viewpoint," *Journal
of Consumer Research* 35(3):

- Experiment 1: 1,058 towel-reuse opportunities. Standard appeal **35.1%**
  compliance. Descriptive social norm ("almost 75% of guests do help") **44.1%**.
- Experiment 2: 1,595 instances. The **most specific** norm, referencing guests
  "who stayed in *this room*," produced **49.3%** versus 37.2% baseline.

Narrowing a claim from general to specifically-like-you measurably increased
compliance. That is direct experimental backing for the whole be-specific
doctrine, and it is almost never cited in copywriting material.

It is **not** evidence that testimonials lift conversion by any percentage. No
comparably rigorous field experiment on testimonials was located. Cite the
mechanism, never a number.

**Hierarchy, strongest to weakest:**

1. Specific, quantified, third-party-verifiable results
2. Named identifiable customers with photo, full name, title
3. Independent third-party validation (press, review platforms)
4. Demonstrations: screenshots, GIFs, video, live data
5. Guarantees and risk reversal (anxiety reduction, not positive proof)
6. Generic unattributed testimonials
7. Vanity counts and logo bars

Security and certification badges have decayed to *expected* rather than
persuasive: their absence now hurts more than their presence helps.

Why third-party proof outranks self-authored claims, per CXL: "You are not your
own best salesperson; the people your audience already trust are."

**Rule:** proof sits adjacent to the claim it supports. If the user supplies no
proof, weaken the claim. Never invent a customer, number, or testimonial.

## Objections

Surfacing methods: customer and ex-customer interviews, surveys on scenarios and
anxieties, review mining (Amazon, Reddit, TripAdvisor, Yelp), heatmaps,
post-conversion polls, competitor audits specifically for how rivals handle
objections.

**Interview technique worth using verbatim:** convert every "why" into a "what."
"Why did you do that?" becomes "What was the thought process behind that
decision?" Why-questions push people into self-justification. Also: do not ask
customers what they want, because "most customers aren't very good at coming up
with innovative solutions." Objections surface by reconstructing the purchase
story, not by direct questioning.

**Sequencing rule:** objections resolve **before the ask they block**, never
after. The solution-introduction section is built around the number one
objection and sits before product detail, price, and close.

An FAQ is where objections go to be ignored. Use it for logistics, not for the
objection that decides the sale.

## CTA

**Calibration first.** Only about **1 in 7 CTA tests produce a statistically
significant result**, though those that do average a 49% lift (Wingify data via
CXL). Most CTA tweaks are noise. Let that temper every confident CTA claim.

- **Specificity ladder:** "Submit" → "Learn More" → "Get instant access to the
  free report." Operating frame: perceived value of the click must exceed
  perceived cost of the click.
- **Throwaway button copy is a named failure.** The button is the site of
  conversion. Write it as deliberately as the headline.
- **CXL refuses to give a formula:** "a good CTA draws heavily on the context of
  the page." CXL runs multiple differently-worded CTAs to serve varying reader
  readiness.
- One primary action per page. Attention ratio toward 1:1. Note: attention ratio
  is design doctrine, its source cites no data.
- Friction reduction belongs adjacent to the button, where the hesitation happens.

**No numeric repetition cadence exists.** Practitioners treat placement as
judgment. Do not manufacture a rule.

**Form friction, real data.** Baymard 2024: average checkout is 5.1 steps and
11.3 fields, down from 12.7 in 2019; benchmark is roughly 8 fields; 17% of users
have abandoned a checkout due to complexity. Baymard publishes **no**
single-number lift claim for field reduction.

**Speed.** Deloitte Digital, *Milliseconds Make Millions* (2020), 37 brands, ~30
million sessions: a 0.1s mobile improvement associated with +8.4% retail
conversions. Observational and vendor-commissioned, not an RCT. Frame it that way.

**Testing guardrail.** Evan Miller, "How Not to Run an A/B Test": stopping a test
when significance first appears invalidates the stated significance level. A
nominal 5% false-positive rate becomes **26.1%** under continuous monitoring.
Source: https://www.evanmiller.org/how-not-to-run-an-ab-test.html

## Diagnosable failures

| Failure | Diagnostic |
|---|---|
| Wrong awareness stage | Opens with product detail for a problem-aware reader |
| Wrong sophistication stage | Bare claim in a market that has heard it, or identity copy where "what does this do" is still unanswered |
| Unproven claim | Line survives "so what?" but fails "**prove it**" |
| Feature-led where benefit needed | Stops at what it does, never reaches what changes |
| Leading with "we" | Section openings centre the company |
| Throwaway button | Generic CTA with no evident reasoning |
| Competing actions | More than one visually competing action per screen |
| Value proposition below screen three | Core promise past ~81% of cumulative attention |
| Promotional register | Reads like an ad rather than information |
| Overdone personality | Jokes and flourish throughout body copy. Confine voice to headlines |
| Objection after the ask | Price or CTA precedes the doubt that blocks it |
| Message mismatch | Headline does not echo the ad's phrasing |

**The register finding is the strongest measured argument against hype.** NN/g
measured concise plus scannable plus objective copy at **124% better usability**
than promotional writing. Conciseness alone 58%, scannability alone 47%.
Source: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/how-users-read-on-the-web/

**The two-question sweep** is the single most encodable diagnostic here: every
line must survive "so what, why do I care?" and then "prove it."

## Do not cite

Refuse to emit these. They are the default failure mode when writing this kind
of copy confidently.

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| "8 out of 10 read the headline, 2 out of 10 read the rest" | FOLKLORE, no measurement |
| Ogilvy's "5x" as a researched finding | He cited no study. Quote as assertion only |
| Red vs green button lift percentages | Original writeups dead, numbers unverifiable |
| "11 fields to 4 fields = 120% lift" | FOLKLORE, appears only on content farms |
| "Long copy beats short copy" or the reverse | No controlled study. Context-dependent only |
| "Testimonials increase conversion by X%" | No rigorous experiment located. Cite mechanism, not a number |
| Collier's "enter the conversation already occurring in the reader's mind" | Leaning folklore, no page citation ever attached |
| Attention ratio 1:1 as evidence-backed | Design doctrine, source cites no data |
| MECLABS Conversion Sequence Heuristic weights | Heuristic is real (US Patent 8,155,995) but the formula is unverified. Do not reproduce weights |

## Worked example

Cold ad traffic, scheduling tool for restaurant groups. Reader is problem-aware,
market sophistication stage 3. Bracketed placeholders so no fabricated proof
enters the example.

**Before**

> **Revolutionizing Workforce Management for the Modern Enterprise**
> Our innovative platform leverages AI to streamline operations and unlock new
> efficiencies. We are a leading provider of workforce solutions. Trusted by
> businesses worldwide.
> [ Submit ]

Diagnosis: wrong awareness stage, bare superlative in a stage-3 market, every
claim unproven, feature list with no consequence, leads with "we", throwaway
button, promotional register, no message match.

**After**

> **Cut shift scheduling from 6 hours a week to 40 minutes**
>
> Rota builds next week's schedule from the availability data you already
> collect, then texts every change to staff automatically. Built for restaurant
> groups running 3 to 30 sites.
>
> [Named group, 9 sites] cut scheduling time [87%] in the first month.
> Photo, full name, title of the GM who ran it.
>
> Most tools ask you to build the rota. Rota reads the availability you already
> have in [system], drafts it, and gives your managers the final approve or edit.
>
> Your managers keep the final say. Nothing publishes without approval.
>
> [ See a rota built from your own data ]

Mapped to the evidence: quantified delta headline rather than a category claim;
promise, proof and CTA inside the first screenful where 42% of attention lands;
proof specific and named to the reader's own segment, which is the
Goldstein/Cialdini specificity effect; the mechanism paragraph answers
sophistication stage 3 rather than restating the claim; objection resolves before
the ask; button names the next micro-step; register concrete rather than
promotional.

Never ship bracketed numbers you were not given.
