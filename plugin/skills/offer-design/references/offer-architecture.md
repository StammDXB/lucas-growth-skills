# Offer Architecture

Load when: constructing or diagnosing a commercial offer.

An offer is not a price and not a promotion. It is the complete proposition:
what the buyer gets, how it works, why it is believable, what it costs, what
happens if it fails, and why now.

## The governing rule: offers are conditional on market state

**The correct offer for a saturated market is structurally different, not
louder.**

Most offer advice, including the popular frameworks, is stage-specific advice
presented as universal law. Applying a stage-1 offer to a stage-5 market
produces something that reads as naive. The reverse reads as vague.

Read `references/persuasion-frameworks.md` for the awareness and sophistication
ladders. Diagnose the market state **before** choosing an offer structure.

| Market state | What the offer must do |
|---|---|
| First to market | State the promise plainly. It has never been heard. |
| Claim escalating | Enlarge or specify the promise. |
| Claim exhausted | Shift to **mechanism**. How it works becomes the offer. |
| Mechanism copied | Better mechanism, or a new frame. |
| All burned out | Sell identity. Who the buyer becomes, not what they get. |

This single distinction upgrades offer construction from template-filling to
diagnosis.

## The components

Each does a distinct job. A missing component is a hole the buyer falls through.

1. **Core promise.** The specific outcome, in the buyer's terms. Not the feature
   set, not the category.
2. **Mechanism.** Why this delivers the promise when other things did not. In a
   saturated market this *is* the offer.
3. **Proof.** Evidence the mechanism works. See `references/evidence-and-proof.md`
   for the hierarchy. Never fabricate.
4. **Risk reversal.** What happens if it does not work. Guarantees, trials,
   staged payment, exit terms.
5. **Price and terms.** The number, the structure, and the payment shape.
6. **Reason to act now.** Only if one genuinely exists. See below.
7. **Bonuses**, if any. Should remove an objection or complete the outcome, not
   pad perceived value.

**The diagnostic:** name which objection each component removes. Any component
that removes no objection is padding, and padding reads as compensation for a
weak core.

## Urgency has a mechanism, not just an ethic

This is the part most often got wrong, and there is a principled rule for it.

The **Elaboration Likelihood Model** (Petty and Cacioppo) distinguishes central
processing (careful scrutiny of the argument) from peripheral processing (cues
and heuristics). Scarcity is a peripheral cue.

**Consequence:** scarcity works in impulse and low-involvement contexts, where
peripheral processing dominates. In high-consideration purchases, the buyer
scrutinises the cue itself, and a manufactured deadline is read as evidence of
manipulation.

Combined with **Friestad and Wright's persuasion knowledge model**, which
describes how people recognise and correct for persuasion attempts, this
predicts the failure mode precisely: **the more motivated and informed the
buyer, the more urgency backfires.**

So the rule is not "never use urgency." It is:

- **Real constraint, any market:** state it plainly. Cohort start dates, capacity
  limits, genuine expiry.
- **Impulse or low-consideration:** urgency is legitimate and effective.
- **High-consideration or expert buyer:** urgency without a real mechanism costs
  you more than it earns.

If you cannot name the mechanism behind the deadline, there is no deadline.

## Context changes the architecture

| Context | What changes |
|---|---|
| **Luxury and premium** | Discounting is a positioning decision, not a pricing one. Scarcity must be structural (production, allocation) not promotional. See `references/luxury-codes.md`. |
| **Volume and mass** | Price structure and friction reduction do most of the work. Mechanism matters less. |
| **Service** | The offer is largely risk reversal and scope clarity. Buyers fear ambiguity more than price. |
| **Product** | Demonstration outperforms description. Trial beats argument. |
| **High consideration** | Longer proof, explicit objection handling, no artificial urgency. |
| **Impulse** | Compression. One promise, one price, one action. |
| **B2B** | Multiple stakeholders with different objections. The offer must survive being forwarded to someone who did not read the pitch. |

## Guarantees, and the trap in them

A guarantee transfers risk from buyer to seller. That is its job.

**But guarantees create adverse selection when they are misaligned.** A
no-questions refund on an information product selects for people who extract the
value and refund. A guarantee tied to *demonstrated use* ("show me you did the
work and it did not work") selects for genuine buyers and filters the rest.

Design the guarantee around the behaviour you want, not around maximum
reassurance.

## Gotchas

- **Fake scarcity.** A countdown that resets, "only 3 left" that is always 3.
  The short-term conversion gain is real. So is the permanent loss of the right
  to be believed. In luxury it is unrecoverable.
- **Discount dependency.** Repeated discounting teaches the market its real
  price and trains buyers to wait. Michael Kors publicly pulled out of department
  store discounting at an estimated cost of over $150m in annual revenue, on the
  grounds it was "creating confusion in the consumers' mind relative to the value
  of the brand."
- **Bonus padding.** Stacked bonuses of unrelated value signal a weak core offer.
- **Offers that win conversions and damage positioning.** Track both. A promotion
  that lifts this quarter and moves the brand's perceived tier down is a loss.
- **Price presented before value is built.** In considered purchases, the number
  is only interpretable after the mechanism and proof land.
- **Guarantee as decoration.** A guarantee nobody could actually claim, or whose
  terms are hidden, is worse than none. It reads as a trick.
- **"Unique mechanism" cited as a framework.** It is folk terminology with no
  identifiable originator. Use the concept, do not attribute it.
