# Offer Design

An offer is not a price and not a promotion. It is the whole proposition: what
they get, how it works, why it is believable, what it costs, what happens if it
fails, and why now.

Most "the copy isn't converting" problems are offer problems. Better words
cannot fix a proposition the buyer does not want or does not believe.

## Before anything: size the ask

If this is a quick question, a one-line lookup, a terminology check, or a small
task carrying no real stakes, answer it directly and stop. No clarifying
sequence, no rubric, no delivery note, no assumptions block.

The steps below are for real deliverables. Run them on a small question and you
produce ceremony instead of judgment, which is worse than a plain answer.

## Step 1: Clarify before constructing

Do not design an offer on assumptions. Ask if these are not supplied.

Required:
1. **What is actually being sold**, and what does the buyer get concretely?
2. **Who is buying**, and what have they already tried?
3. **What does the market already believe** about this category and these claims?
4. **What proof exists?** Real results, real names, real numbers.
5. **What are the real constraints?** Capacity, margin, delivery, legal.

Also ask:
6. What is the current offer and what specifically is failing about it?
7. Is there any genuine scarcity or deadline, or would it have to be manufactured?

If proof does not exist, say so and build an offer that does not depend on
proof you do not have. Never invent results, customers, or numbers.

## Step 2: Diagnose market state first

This is the step that separates diagnosis from template-filling.

Read `references/persuasion-frameworks.md` and `references/offer-architecture.md`.

Establish **awareness** (what does the buyer know) and **sophistication** (how
many competing claims has this market already heard). The correct offer for a
saturated market is structurally different, not louder.

- Claim exhausted → the **mechanism** becomes the offer
- Mechanism copied → a better mechanism, or a reframe
- Everything burned out → sell identity, not outcome

## Step 3: Build the components

Each removes a specific objection. Name which one, for each.

| Component | Job |
|---|---|
| Core promise | The specific outcome in the buyer's terms |
| Mechanism | Why this works when the last thing did not |
| Proof | Evidence the mechanism is real |
| Risk reversal | What happens if it fails |
| Price and terms | The number, structure, and payment shape |
| Reason to act now | Only if a real one exists |
| Bonuses | Remove an objection or complete the outcome. Never padding |

Anything that removes no objection is padding, and padding reads as
compensation for a weak core.

## Step 4: Test urgency against the buyer

Do not add urgency by default. Scarcity is a peripheral cue: it works where
buyers process peripherally and backfires where they scrutinise.

- **Real constraint?** State it plainly. Always legitimate.
- **Impulse or low-consideration?** Urgency is effective.
- **High-consideration or expert buyer?** Manufactured urgency costs more than
  it earns. The more motivated the buyer, the worse it performs.

If you cannot name the mechanism behind the deadline, there is no deadline. Say
so rather than inventing one.

## Step 5: Check the offer does not damage positioning

An offer can win this quarter and cost the brand a tier. Both are real outcomes.

Check specifically:
- Does this train buyers to wait for a discount?
- Would a premium buyer read this as cheapening?
- Does the guarantee select for the customers you want, or against them?
- If this ran every quarter for two years, what would the brand be worth?

For luxury and premium, read `references/luxury-codes.md` before recommending
any discount or promotional scarcity.

## Step 6: Validate

Load `references/rubric-offer-design.md`. Score every line PASS or FAIL with
quoted evidence. Burden of proof is on PASS. Fix, re-score, **maximum two
rounds**. State anything that still fails.

Self-check against a written standard, not independent review.

## Step 7: Deliver

```
The offer          promise, mechanism, proof, risk reversal, price, terms
Why it works       which objection each component removes
What it costs you  margin, delivery load, positioning implications
The risk           honestly, what could go wrong
Open               what you assumed, what proof is still needed
```

Keep the note short. Do not show the rubric or the revision rounds.

## Gotchas

- **Treating a copy problem as an offer problem, or the reverse.** If the
  proposition is right and the words are wrong, fix the words. If nobody wants
  the thing on these terms, no copy will save it. Diagnose which.
- **Fake scarcity.** Short-term gain, permanent loss of the right to be believed.
- **Discount dependency.** Teaches the market your real price and trains buyers
  to wait.
- **Guarantee causing adverse selection.** A no-questions refund on an
  information product selects for people who extract and refund. Tie the
  guarantee to demonstrated use.
- **Bonus stacking.** Unrelated bonuses signal a weak core.
- **Price before value in a considered purchase.** The number is uninterpretable
  before the mechanism and proof land.
- **Copying a framework across market states.** Most popular offer advice is
  stage-specific advice presented as universal law.
