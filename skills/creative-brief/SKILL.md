# Creative Brief

A brief is a decision document, not a summary. Its job is to make the hard calls
*before* anyone makes anything, so the work can be judged against something
other than taste.

Most bad creative traces to a brief that avoided a decision. The team then
argued about the work when they should have argued about the brief.

## Step 1: Interrogate before writing

A brief written from an unexamined request inherits every ambiguity in it. Ask
until you can answer these. Do not fill gaps with plausible-sounding assumptions.

Required:
1. **What is the business problem?** Not the deliverable requested. A request for
   "a video" is a solution someone already chose. Find out what it is for.
2. **Who specifically**, and what do they currently think or do?
3. **What must change?** From what, to what. Behaviour, not sentiment.
4. **How will we know it worked?** Before the work exists.
5. **What is true about the brand or product** that supports this?
6. **What are the real constraints?** Budget, timing, channels, legal, mandatories.

Also ask:
7. **What is deliberately out of scope?**
8. **Who approves, and what will they object to?** Unnamed approvers are the most
   common cause of late-stage collapse.
9. **What has been tried and failed?**

If the user cannot answer 1 to 4, say so plainly. A brief built on those gaps
will produce work nobody can evaluate. Offer to help find the answers instead of
proceeding.

## Step 2: Make the decisions

The brief must **exclude** things. Test every section by asking what it rules
out. Anything that rules nothing out is padding.

The one hard call: **one proposition**. A brief carrying two ideas produces work
that does neither, and the debate you avoided at brief stage returns at
presentation stage with a bigger audience.

Note on terminology: "single-minded proposition" is standard agency vocabulary,
but no authoritative published definition was located and the Account Planning
Group's own explainer does not define it. Use the concept, do not cite it as a
formal framework.

## Step 3: Write it

```
The problem          the business problem in one sentence, not the deliverable
The audience         who, specifically, and what they currently believe or do
The behaviour change from what, to what
The proposition      one sentence, internal, not copy
Reason to believe    why the audience should accept it
What we will not do  explicit exclusions
Mandatories          legal, brand, channel, format requirements
Success              how we will know, defined before the work exists
Constraints          budget, timing, approvers by name
```

Keep it to one page. A brief that needs a second page is usually two briefs, or
one brief plus a background document that should be attached rather than inlined.

**Write the proposition for the team, not the audience.** If it reads like a
tagline, it is doing the wrong job.

## Step 4: Pressure-test

Run these before handing it over.

- **The disagreement test.** Could someone reasonably argue for a different
  proposition? If not, you have described the category rather than made a choice.
- **The substitution test.** Put a competitor's name on the brief. If it still
  works, it is not briefing *this* brand.
- **The two-directions test.** Could two good teams produce genuinely different
  work from this? If only one obvious answer exists, the brief is a spec. If a
  hundred equally valid answers exist, it is too loose.
- **The judgment test.** When the work comes back, what would you point at to say
  it is off-brief? If nothing, the brief cannot be used to judge.
- **The approver test.** Would the named approver recognise their own concern in
  this? If not, expect a late reversal.

## Step 5: Validate

Load `references/rubric-creative-brief.md`. Score every line PASS or FAIL with
quoted evidence. Burden of proof on PASS. Fix, re-score, **maximum two rounds**.

## Step 6: Deliver

The brief itself, then two lines:

```
Open        what you assumed, and what still needs answering
Risk        the most likely way this brief produces the wrong work
```

Naming the risk is not pessimism. It is the most useful line in the document,
because it tells the team where to concentrate.

## Interrogating an existing brief

When asked to review rather than write:

1. **What decision is missing?** Usually the proposition, the exclusion, or the
   success measure.
2. **Where does it hedge?** "Modern but traditional", "bold yet approachable".
   Every hedge is a decision deferred to whoever makes the work.
3. **What can it not judge?** Name the work it would fail to reject.
4. **Who is unnamed?** Missing approvers are missing objections.

Give the diagnosis before the rewrite. If the brief has a hole, patching the
prose hides it.

## Gotchas

- **The deliverable presented as the problem.** "We need a brochure" is an answer
  somebody already chose. Brief the problem.
- **Two propositions.** The most common and most expensive failure.
- **Adjective hedging.** "Premium but accessible" is two briefs pretending to be
  one.
- **Success defined after the work.** Then the work defines success, and it always
  passes.
- **Audience as demographic.** Age and income are not a belief or a behaviour.
- **No exclusions.** A brief that permits everything judges nothing.
- **Background dumped inline.** Context is not a brief. Attach it.
