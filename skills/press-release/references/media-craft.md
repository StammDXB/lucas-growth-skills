# Press Release and Media Craft

Load when: writing a press release, media pitch, or press materials.

**Evidence note.** Nearly every "journalists say they want X" statistic in
circulation traces to Muck Rack's *State of Journalism* or Cision's *State of
the Media*. Both companies sell pitch-distribution software to PR buyers, and
both surveys are self-selected samples recruited through the vendor's own
journalist userbase. That is a structural conflict. Treat all such percentages
as vendor-commissioned and unverified.

## The reframe

You are not announcing. You are supplying a journalist with a story they can
publish, in a form that costs them the least work.

**The acceptance test has changed, and this is the most actionable finding
here.** Reuters Institute 2025 (n=326 digital leaders, 51 countries) found
Facebook referral traffic down **67%** over two years, X down **50%**, and
**77%** of publishers ranking subscription and membership as their top revenue
priority, ahead of display advertising.

When subscription is the priority and platform referrals have collapsed, an
editor's test shifts from "will this get clicks" to **"will this be worth
something to a person who already pays us."** Traffic-bait announcements lose
value. Material that helps a paying reader make a decision gains value.

Also from that survey: **74%** of publishers are worried about AI-generated
search summaries cannibalising traffic. Measure coverage on credibility and
citation, not referral traffic.

## Newsworthiness

### The citation traps

**Galtung and Ruge is real:** "The Structure of Foreign News," *Journal of Peace
Research*, Vol. 2, No. 1, **1965**.

Two errors to avoid, both common because a widely used teaching page contains
them: the year is **not 1973**, and the name is not "Gatlung." That page also
prints a **ten-item** list where the paper is generally described as proposing
twelve.

**Harcup and O'Neill are real, and there are two papers:** 2001, "What Is News?
Galtung and Ruge revisited," and **2017**, "What is News? News values revisited
(again)," both *Journalism Studies*.

**Do not state their item lists.** The papers were confirmed to exist with
correct citations, but the full texts were paywalled and the contents could not
be obtained. Any list you have seen attributed to them online is unverified.

### The commercially usable rubric

The academic frameworks describe *foreign news selection*, not corporate
announcements. Most news values are structurally unavailable to a company. The
job is to identify which two or three are genuinely in play.

**Reliably available to a company:**
- **Impact.** A number of people affected, or a magnitude of money, time, or risk changed. The workhorse.
- **Timeliness.** A genuine date-anchored trigger.
- **Proximity.** Geographic or sector adjacency to that outlet's defined audience.
- **Continuity.** You are a new development in a story the outlet already runs. The cheapest genuine route to coverage.

**Available but usually faked:**
- **Prominence.** Only counts if the person is prominent *to that outlet's readers*. Your CEO is not. A regulator or a named institution can be.
- **Conflict.** Real only with an identifiable counterparty who would object. Disagreeing with an abstraction is not conflict.
- **Novelty.** Real only if the reporter cannot name a comparable prior instance.

**Structurally unavailable:**
- **Composition.** The editor's need to balance the issue. You cannot influence it, only lose to it. This is why a good release still gets killed.

> **Hard gate.** Score against Impact, Timeliness, Proximity, Continuity,
> Prominence, Conflict, Novelty. **Fewer than two means it is not a press
> release.** It is a blog post, a customer email, or a LinkedIn post. Route it
> there. The highest-leverage act in media relations is refusing to issue.
>
> **The "wish it were news" test:** for each claimed value, name the specific
> external party for whom it is true. "Impact: 4,000 hospitality workers," not
> "Impact: the industry." If you cannot name the party, the value is absent.

## Structure

**Inverted pyramid.** Most important first, descending, so an editor can cut
from the bottom. The telegraph rationale is dead; the cut-from-the-bottom
rationale is stronger than ever, because now it is the reporter cutting your
release for parts.

**The lede, to spec:** 25 to 30 words, **rarely more than 40**. One sentence
preferred. Active voice. Lead with the single most important W, not all six.
"A lead is an implicit promise to your readers."

This is the most mechanically checkable rule in the discipline. Count the words.

**The nut graf.** Origin: the Wall Street Journal under managing editor **Barney
Kilgore, 1941**. Poynter defines it as "a paragraph that says what this whole
story is about and why you should read it," answering "why should I bother with
this story?"

In a summary-lead release it is paragraph two and does the "so what, and why
now" work. **Most corporate releases skip from announcement straight to quote,
which is exactly where the nut graf belongs.**

Note: one widely used teaching page uses "nut graphs" to mean all body
paragraphs. That is an idiosyncrasy and conflicts with the standard definition.
Say "body paragraphs" for those.

**Dateline.** City in caps, state abbreviation, at the start. Semi-ritual, but
cheap and diagnostic: a correct one signals wire experience, a wrong one signals
the opposite.

**Boilerplate.** Not read by reporters. It exists so a desk can describe the
company without a second call. Cap at 60 to 75 words, place below the contact
block, and **never let it carry a load-bearing fact**.

**Media contact separate from general company contact.** Genuinely load-bearing.
Routing a reporter to an enquiries inbox is a common and fatal failure. Named
human, direct mobile, stated monitoring window with timezone.

**Notes to Editors**, after the contact block, for supplementary material and
available photography. Real and under-used.

**Dead ritual, do not encode:** double-spacing and wide margins. Those existed so
an editor could annotate a faxed page.

**Length:** one page target, two pages ceiling. Beyond two it reads as an
article, not a release.

Two length claims to reject: a widely cited teaching page says "no more than 50
words," which is a lede, not a release, and is contradicted two paragraphs later
on the same page. And "300 to 400 words" has no traceable primary source.

## Quotes

**The failure is structural, not stylistic.** The corporate quote is written by
committee and approved by legal. Legal's job is to remove anything falsifiable.
**A quote with nothing falsifiable in it has, by construction, no information
content.** Nobody owns the output quality.

### Four tests every quote must pass

1. **Attribution swap.** Replace the speaker and company with a competitor's. If
   it still reads as true, it says nothing. Kill it.
2. **Falsifiability.** Could an informed person disagree with this sentence? A
   quote must contain a claim, number, causal assertion, or contestable judgment.
3. **Paraphrase.** Would a reporter quote this, or paraphrase it? If it contains
   only facts, they will paraphrase and the quote is wasted. **Facts go in the
   body; judgment goes in the quote.**
4. **Said aloud.** Read it out. If no human speaks in those clauses, no reporter
   will present it as speech.

### What a usable quote does

Answers a question the facts raise but do not settle. One of five:
- **Causation.** Why this happened, when the reason is not obvious.
- **Consequence.** What changes for a named third party.
- **Trade-off or admission.** What you gave up. **Admissions are the most
  quotable corporate speech that exists, and are almost never offered.**
- **Contested judgment.** A position an informed peer would argue with.
- **Concrete prediction.** Falsifiable, with a date.

### Banned on sight

thrilled, excited, delighted, proud, honored, game-changer, revolutionary,
groundbreaking, disruptive, best-in-class, world-class, leading provider,
industry-leading, innovative, cutting-edge, next-generation, seamless, robust,
empower, leverage, passionate about, committed to, unlock, unleash, at the
forefront, ecosystem, solutions, journey, "we are pleased to announce", sets a
new standard, raises the bar, poised to.

Also kill any quote whose main verb is "to be" plus an emotion adjective, and any
quote describing the announcement rather than its consequences.

> **Rule:** one quote, two maximum. A second quote from a second executive at the
> same company adds nothing and should be cut by default. A quote from a
> customer, partner, or independent third party is worth more than any number of
> internal ones.

## Pitch versus release

| | Pitch | Release |
|---|---|---|
| Job | Persuade one named person a story exists | Be the citable record of what was announced |
| Audience | One reporter | Anyone, including the archive |
| Voice | Yours, first person, to a person | Neutral, third person |
| Success | A reply | Accurate enough to quote without a call |
| Failure | Generic, unaddressed, too long | Vague, unverifiable, no news value |

> **Routing rule.** Never send a release with no pitch. Never send a pitch that
> is a pasted release. If the email body *is* the release, you have told the
> recipient you did not think about them.

**Pitch checklist**, framed as mechanism rather than statistic:
- Subject line states the news, not the ask. Not "Story idea," not "Press release:".
- Short enough to read without scrolling on a phone.
- Paragraph one: why this reporter, naming their specific prior work.
- Paragraph two: the news in one sentence, in lede shape.
- Paragraph three: what you can supply. Access, data, exclusive, images.
- No attachments on first contact. Link out.
- One follow-up maximum, and only with something new.

**Channel discipline.** A press release is not a tip. Newsroom tips
infrastructure exists for sources with information someone does not want
published, and is built around encrypted, individual contact. TechCrunch's tips
page, for instance, routes press releases to an **unencrypted** address in a
single sentence while the rest of the page concerns secure source protection.
Sending a promotional release into a source-protection channel is a credibility
event, not a neutral one.

## Embargo and exclusive

Documented failure patterns, from Embargo Watch (Ivan Oransky):

1. **Specify the timezone, every time, in every communication.** Timezone
   ambiguity, especially around daylight saving transitions, is the most
   documented cause of embargo failure. Write "09:00 GST (05:00 UTC), Tuesday 12
   May." Never "9am Tuesday."
2. **You are the most likely party to break your own embargo.** The Max Planck
   Institute announced a PNAS study days before its embargo lifted. Before
   sending, audit every channel that could publish early: your newsroom page,
   scheduled social, an investor deck, a partner blog, a conference programme.
3. **A break is usually unrecoverable and you lift for everyone.** The Journal of
   Experimental Biology lifted early after the Guardian published a study ahead of
   time. Pre-make that decision before sending.
4. **Never re-embargo.** PLOS ONE tried after a typhoon delay and reversed.
5. **An independently reported story is not a break.** A reporter who finds it
   themselves owes you nothing. Pat Anstett was blacklisted in 2002 for a story
   obtained through independent reporting. That is a documented and costly error.

**Consequences fall on the journalist and are real.** Reuters had press
credentials revoked at the American College of Cardiology's next annual meeting
after breaking embargoes on cardiology studies.

**When each fits.** An embargo is a gift of preparation time: appropriate when
material genuinely needs reporting time and multiple outlets should publish
together. If the story takes ten minutes to write, an embargo gives nothing and
adds risk. An exclusive trades reach for depth, and its real cost is relational:
the reporters who did not get it know. Never offer the same exclusive twice.
Never use the words interchangeably.

**Listed companies:** any embargo or exclusive involving material nonpublic
information goes to counsel before it goes to a reporter. US selective-disclosure
rules bear directly on this and the specifics were not verifiable here.

## The SEO question is settled, and not in PR's favour

Google's Spam Policies name press releases explicitly under **Link Spam**,
verbatim:

> "Advertorials or native advertising where payment is received for articles
> that include links that pass ranking credit, or **links with optimized anchor
> text in articles, guest posts, or press releases distributed on other sites.**"

Google also states that buying and selling links is not itself a violation "as
long as they are qualified with a `rel="nofollow"` or `rel="sponsored"`
attribute value," and that such links "will generally not be followed."

> **Unambiguous routing rule.** Paid wire distribution is a paid placement. Its
> links must carry `sponsored` or `nofollow` and therefore **pass no ranking
> credit by design**. Optimized anchor text in a distributed release is a stated
> policy violation. **Never justify a release on link-equity grounds.** If a
> client or vendor proposes it, that is a policy violation, not a tactic.

What remains legitimate: brand-name search visibility, the release living on your
own domain as a citable record, and discoverability by a journalist or analyst
researching you.

Note: Google separately states that wire service and press release service sites
are **not** examples of site reputation abuse. Distribution itself is legitimate.
Anchor-text optimisation within it is not.

**Wire distribution generally:** with the link rationale eliminated, the
remaining value is narrow. Regulatory or disclosure obligations, guaranteed
simultaneous timestamped publication, and syndication onto financial terminals.
For a company with no disclosure obligation, the case is weak and should be
argued explicitly rather than assumed.

## Open question, do not adjudicate

Whether AI-generated answers make earned media *more* valuable (a trusted, cited
source models weight heavily) or *less* (summaries absorb the coverage and strip
attribution). Reuters Institute documents publisher anxiety, not a resolved
outcome. Present it as open and refuse to assert a direction.

## Diagnosable failures

1. **No news value.** Scores on fewer than two. The dominant failure. Fix: do not issue.
2. **Lede buries the news or busts the word count.** 25 to 30 words, 40 max.
3. **No nut graf.** Nothing says why to care or why now.
4. **Unusable quotes.** Fails attribution-swap or falsifiability.
5. **Unverifiable claims.** "Leading", "first", "fastest-growing" with no source. "First" is checkable and being caught wrong is expensive.
6. **Material omission.** What did you leave out that changes the meaning?
7. **Contact routing failure.** No named media contact, or a general inbox.
8. **The company is the subject of every sentence.** The audience appears only as beneficiary.
9. **Wrong artifact.** Pitch pasted as release, or release sent as pitch.
10. **Timezone-ambiguous embargo.**
11. **Self-broken embargo.** Your own channels publish first.
12. **Anchor-text link stuffing.** A stated Google policy violation.
13. **Passive voice throughout.** Conceals agency, reads as evasion.

## Worked example

**Fictional. Invented names and figures for illustration only.**

**Before**

> **Meridian Announces Groundbreaking New Platform to Revolutionize Guest Engagement**
> DUBAI — Meridian Hospitality Group, a leading provider of innovative
> hospitality solutions, today announced the launch of its groundbreaking new
> platform, GuestFlow, set to revolutionize how hotels interact with guests.
> "We are thrilled and excited," said the CEO. "This is a real game-changer. We
> are passionate about delivering world-class solutions."

Diagnosis: promotional headline; 41-word lede whose news content is "a company
launched a product"; news values score zero of seven; every quote sentence passes
the attribution-swap test, meaning it says nothing; twelve banned terms; no nut
graf; no media contact.

**After**

> **Meridian cuts hotel check-in queues by routing arrivals through WhatsApp, after 11-month trial across 14 UAE properties**
>
> DUBAI, 12 May 2026 — Fourteen UAE hotels cut average check-in wait times from
> 9 minutes to under 2 by moving arrival paperwork to WhatsApp before guests
> reached the property, according to trial results published today.
>
> The trial matters because UAE hotels are absorbing record arrival volumes with
> roughly the same front-desk headcount they had in 2023. Meridian ran it across
> 14 properties from June 2025 to April 2026, covering 340,000 arrivals.
>
> Adoption was uneven. Guests booking through OTAs used the pre-arrival flow 71%
> of the time; guests on corporate rates used it 34%.
>
> "We assumed the barrier was that guests wouldn't want to do paperwork on their
> phone. It wasn't. On a corporate booking we often don't have the guest's
> number, only the booker's," said [Name, title]. "That is a data problem, not a
> product problem, and we have not solved it."
>
> Two of the 14 properties saw no improvement. Both operate a single-desk
> arrivals hall where the queue is constrained by lift capacity, not paperwork.
>
> **Media contact:** [named person], [direct mobile]. Monitored 08:00 to 20:00
> GST through 16 May.
>
> Ends

**The highest-value edit is the disclosed failure.** Two of fourteen properties
saw no improvement, with the reason. It is the one thing a competitor's PR team
would never write, it is why a reporter will believe the other numbers, and it
costs nothing that was not going to surface anyway.

Never ship bracketed figures you were not given.
