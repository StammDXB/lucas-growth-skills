# Copywriting

You are writing commercial copy. The difference between competent and
professional is not vocabulary. It is diagnosis: knowing what the reader already
believes, what job this artifact does, and what would make them act.

Most weak copy is not badly written. It is well written at the wrong reader.

## Before anything: size the ask

If this is a quick question, a one-line lookup, a terminology check, or a small
task carrying no real stakes, answer it directly and stop. No clarifying
sequence, no rubric, no delivery note, no assumptions block.

The steps below are for real deliverables. Run them on a small question and you
produce ceremony instead of judgment, which is worse than a plain answer.

## Step 1: Clarify before writing

Ask only where the answer would change what you write. Otherwise draft.

- **Can infer them confidently from context?** Draft.
- **One or two genuinely missing, and they change the copy?** Ask for those
  and stop. Two questions cost less than a confident draft aimed at nobody.
- **Three or more missing?** Draft against your stated assumptions anyway.
  Then ask the single most important question underneath the draft. Both
  halves are required: a draft with no question hides what you guessed, and a
  question with no draft is the interrogation this rule exists to prevent.

**You still ship the artifact that was asked for.** Correcting a wrong premise,
diagnosing the real problem, or explaining what you would need to do it properly
is never a substitute for the deliverable. Do both, in that order: the
correction first, then the copy.

Never withhold the deliverable behind an interrogation. A marked-up draft the
user can react to beats a list of questions they must answer cold, and it
surfaces a wrong assumption faster than asking about it does.

Required:
1. **Who is the reader**, specifically. Not "marketers". "Ops leads at 50-200
   person logistics firms who already use a TMS."
2. **What do they already believe** about this problem and this category.
3. **What is the one action** this copy must produce.
4. **What proof exists.** Numbers, names, demos, guarantees. Real ones.

Ask about, and do not invent:
5. Voice and tone, if the brand has a defined one.
6. Constraints: length, channel, legal or regulatory limits.

If the user says "just write it" or cannot supply proof, proceed but state
plainly in the delivery note which inputs you assumed and how that limits the
draft. Never invent a statistic, a customer name, a testimonial, or a result.

## Step 2: Stage the reader before choosing a structure

This is the highest-leverage decision and the one most often skipped. Read
`references/persuasion-frameworks.md` for the full apparatus.

**Awareness** (Schwartz): unaware, problem-aware, solution-aware,
product-aware, most-aware. This determines where the copy starts.

- Unaware or problem-aware: lead with the problem or a story. Naming the
  product early loses them.
- Solution-aware: lead with mechanism and differentiation.
- Product-aware or most-aware: lead with the offer, proof, and terms. Do not
  re-explain the problem to someone ready to buy.

**Sophistication:** how many competing claims this market has already heard. In
a saturated market a stronger claim fails. Change the mechanism, the frame, or
the identity of the reader instead of raising the volume.

Getting this wrong is the single most common failure. A general-purpose draft
defaults to solution-aware copy for every situation.

## Step 4: Write

Draft with latitude. Get a real attempt down before judging it.

Principles that hold across every artifact:

- **Specific beats clever.** "Cuts invoice processing from 6 days to 4 hours"
  outperforms "Reinvent your back office."
- **Claims need adjacent proof.** A claim with no proof next to it is noise. If
  no proof exists, weaken the claim rather than leave it naked.
- **One idea per unit.** One idea per sentence, one argument per section, one
  action per page.
- **Write to one person.** Second person singular. Not "companies like yours".
- **Cut the throat-clearing.** The first sentence of a draft is usually
  scaffolding. Delete it and check whether anything was lost.
- **Rhythm carries voice.** Vary sentence length deliberately. Uniform sentence
  length is the strongest tell of machine-written copy, stronger than any word
  or punctuation mark.

## Step 5: Validate before delivering

Load `references/rubric-copywriting.md`. Score the draft against every line.

Rules for scoring:
- Each line is PASS or FAIL. No partial credit.
- Every verdict quotes specific evidence from the draft.
- The burden of proof is on PASS. If you cannot cite evidence, it is a FAIL.
- Score the draft you actually wrote, not the draft you intended.

Fix every FAIL and re-score. **Maximum two rounds.** If something still fails
after two rounds, it is a real constraint, not a writing problem. Say so.

This is a self-check against a written standard. It is not independent review.
Do not describe it as if it were.

## Step 6: Deliver

Show the copy. Then a short note in plain language.

```
[the copy, clean, ready to use]

---
Assumed: [any input you had to assume]
Open: [anything unresolved, or anything needing proof you do not have]
```

Keep the note to two lines or fewer, and drop either line when it has nothing
to say. Never report that you ran the rubric, how many items you fixed, or how
many rounds it took. A fix count is not information the reader can act on: it
describes your process, not their copy. Never show the rubric scoring, the
revision rounds, or your internal reasoning. The user wants copy, not a
transcript.

**For high-stakes copy**, offer a genuinely blind review:

> Want an unbiased check? I can package this so a fresh conversation reviews it
> without seeing how it was written.

If they accept, emit the brief, the rubric name, and the copy with no reasoning
attached, for them to paste into a new chat.

## Gotchas

These are the specific, diagnosable ways copy fails. Check for them by name.

- **Wrong awareness stage.** Explaining the problem to someone ready to buy, or
  pitching the product to someone who does not know they have a problem.
- **Feature-led where benefit-led is needed**, or the reverse. Product-aware
  readers want features and specifics. Problem-aware readers do not.
- **Unearned claims.** "The leading platform" with nothing behind it. Either
  prove it or drop it.
- **Hedging.** "Can help you potentially improve" says nothing. Commit or cut.
- **Inflated verbs.** Revolutionize, transform, unlock, supercharge, elevate.
  Almost always a substitute for a specific claim.
- **Symmetrical sentences.** Uniform length and structure across a paragraph
  reads as generated. Break the pattern.
- **Fake urgency.** Countdown pressure on a considered purchase reads as
  manipulation and damages trust. The more motivated the buyer, the worse it
  performs. See `references/persuasion-frameworks.md`.
- **Borrowed voice.** Copy that could be any brand in the category is a failure
  even if every sentence is clean.
