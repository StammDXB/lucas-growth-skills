# Editing and Critiquing Existing Copy

Load when: the user supplies copy and wants it improved, tightened, critiqued,
or made to sound less generic.

Editing is not rewriting. The job is to enhance the existing argument, not
replace it with your own. If you find yourself producing something structurally
different, stop: that is a rewrite, and you should say so and ask.

## The discipline: one dimension per pass

Multiple focused passes beat one unfocused review. Trying to fix clarity, voice,
proof, and rhythm simultaneously means catching none of them properly.

Run these in order. After each pass, check that your edits did not break an
earlier one.

### Pass 1: Clarity

Can the reader understand it on one read?

Look for: sentences carrying two ideas, unclear pronoun references, jargon,
buried points wrapped in qualifications, assumed knowledge the reader lacks.

Method: read through once and mark unclear spots without fixing anything. Fix
only after you have marked everything. Fixing as you go biases you toward local
repairs and away from structural ones.

### Pass 2: So what

Does every claim answer "why should I care?"

Test: read each statement and literally ask "so what?" If there is no answer, the
line is doing no work.

> "Our platform uses AI-powered analytics."
> So what?
> "Surfaces the accounts about to churn, so you call them before they cancel."

Common failures: feature lists with no benefit bridge, company achievements that
do not help the reader, technical capability with no stated outcome.

### Pass 3: Prove it

Is every claim supported?

Look for: "leading", "best-in-class", "trusted by thousands" with nothing behind
them. Every claim needs proof adjacent to it, not in a testimonial section three
screens later.

When there is no proof available, weaken the claim to something true rather than
leaving an unsupported assertion standing. An honest small claim outperforms an
unbelievable large one.

### Pass 4: Specificity

Replace abstractions with concrete detail.

- "Significantly faster" becomes "6 days to 4 hours"
- "Many customers" becomes "340 teams" or the real number
- "Leading companies" becomes the names, if you have permission

If the user has not supplied the specifics, flag the gap. Do not invent a number
to make the sentence better. A fabricated statistic is a worse failure than a
vague one.

### Pass 5: Voice

Is it consistent throughout?

Read aloud. Mark where the register shifts. Copy typically starts in the
intended voice and drifts toward neutral corporate register by the last third,
so check the ending specifically.

See `voice-and-tone.md` for the four-dimension instrument.

### Pass 6: Rhythm

Vary sentence length deliberately.

Count words per sentence across the longest paragraph. If they cluster in a
narrow band, the copy reads as machine-generated regardless of content quality.
This is the strongest AI tell, stronger than any individual word.

Fix by breaking one long sentence into two, and by letting one sentence run.

### Pass 7: Risk

What would stop this reader acting?

Surface the objections and check they are handled at the point they arise, not
deferred to an FAQ. The strongest objection usually belongs immediately after the
main claim, not at the end.

## Word-level checks

Run these as a final scan.

**Cut on sight, unless justified:** revolutionize, transform, unlock,
supercharge, elevate, empower, seamless, robust, cutting-edge, game-changing,
leverage, utilize, solutions, innovative.

**Hedges that void the sentence:** can help, may improve, potentially, designed
to, aims to, could enable. Either commit or cut.

**Throat-clearing openers:** "In today's fast-paced world", "As businesses
increasingly", "It's no secret that". The real first line is usually the second
or third.

**Weak constructions:** "There is / there are" openings, nominalizations
("provide assistance to" for "help"), passive voice where the actor matters.

## Critiquing rather than editing

When asked to critique rather than fix, do not silently rewrite. Structure it:

1. **What works** and specifically why, quoting the line.
2. **The single biggest problem**, with the diagnosis, not just the symptom.
   "The headline is weak" is useless. "The headline addresses a problem-aware
   reader, but this is retargeting traffic who already know the product" is a
   diagnosis.
3. **Prioritized fixes**, most leverage first.
4. **What you would leave alone**, and why. A critique that finds everything
   wrong is not a critique, it is a rewrite in disguise.

## Gotchas

- **Do not flatten voice into correctness.** Distinctive copy often breaks rules
  deliberately. A fragment, a one-word paragraph, or a run-on can be doing real
  work. Only change it if it is failing, not because it is irregular.
- **Do not edit toward your own preferences.** The test is whether it works for
  this reader, not whether you would have written it that way.
- **Preserve the author's argument.** If you disagree with the strategy, say so
  separately. Do not smuggle a different argument in through line edits.
