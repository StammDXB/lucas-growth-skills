# Long-Form Brand and Campaign Copy

Load when: writing a manifesto, About page, campaign copy, brand story, or any
long piece whose job is belief rather than immediate action.

The distinguishing feature of this form is that **nobody has to read it**. A
landing page interrupts someone mid-task. Long-form is chosen. That changes what
earns attention: not urgency, but the promise that the reader will end up
somewhere they could not have got to alone.

## What long-form is actually for

Not conversion. Belief change.

Use it when the reader has to hold a new idea before any offer can make sense:
a category being redefined, a position that contradicts the obvious, a brand
whose value is not self-evident from the product.

**If the job can be done in 200 words, do it in 200 words.** Length is a cost
you pay for a reason. Long-form written because "brands should have a story" is
the most common waste in this form.

## Structure is argument, not format

A long piece is a sequence of beliefs, each one earning the next. Write the
belief sequence before the prose.

1. What does the reader believe now?
2. What do they need to believe by the end?
3. What is the shortest chain of steps between those two states?
4. What would make them reject each step, and what removes that?

If two steps can swap order without loss, one is decoration. If a step cannot be
defended, the argument has a hole no amount of writing will cover.

The most common structural failure is a piece that is *thematically* about
something but makes no argument at all. Test: state the thesis in one sentence.
If you cannot, there is no thesis, only a subject.

## The forms

### Manifesto

A manifesto states what the brand believes and, critically, what it is against.
A manifesto with no enemy is a mood board.

The enemy is usually a practice, a convention, or an accepted compromise, not a
competitor. Naming a competitor is a smaller, meaner move that dates badly.

Requirements:
- A stated position someone could reasonably oppose
- A cost the brand accepts for holding it, which is what makes it credible
- Language the brand can actually live up to operationally

The failure mode is a manifesto nobody in the company could act on differently
tomorrow. If the operations do not change, the manifesto is decoration and staff
will read it as such.

### About page

The most-read and least-considered page on most sites. Visitors arrive with one
of two questions: "can I trust these people" or "who actually are they."

What works: the real origin, the specific decision that shaped the company, the
people with their actual roles, the thing you are deliberately not doing.

What fails: founding-myth inflation, "we believe in excellence," stock language
about passion and innovation, and the passive voice used to hide who did what.

An About page is where proof of character lives, not proof of product. Specifics
about how you work beat adjectives about how you are.

### Campaign copy

Campaign copy serves an idea that exists above it. If the idea is weak, better
copy makes it more visible, not better.

The line's job is to make the idea repeatable and portable. Test it by
separating it from the art: if the line only works with this image, it is a
caption, not a campaign line.

### Brand story

The story explains why this exists in a form a person can retell. The retelling
test is the real bar: if someone cannot repeat it accurately after one read, it
will not travel.

Compress ruthlessly. Most brand stories carry three founders' worth of detail for
an audience that wanted one sentence.

## Craft at length

- **Earn each section.** A reader stops at every section break. Each opening has
  to re-earn the next stretch of attention.
- **Vary the unit.** A long piece of identical paragraph blocks reads as
  homework. Let one section be a single line. Let another run.
- **Concrete before abstract.** Establish the scene, the number, the name, then
  generalise. The reverse order loses people before the abstraction lands.
- **Do not resolve too cleanly.** Long-form that ties every thread into a neat
  lesson reads as manufactured. Real positions have edges and costs.
- **Say the uncomfortable part.** The line the brand would rather omit is
  usually the one that makes the whole piece credible.

## Diagnosable failures

1. **Subject without thesis.** The piece is about something but argues nothing. Diagnostic: you cannot state the thesis in one sentence.
2. **Length without reason.** Diagnostic: the same job is done by the first 200 words.
3. **Manifesto with no enemy.** Diagnostic: no sentence anyone could oppose.
4. **Unlivable claims.** Diagnostic: nothing operational would change if it were true.
5. **Reorderable sections.** Diagnostic: swapping two sections costs nothing.
6. **Abstraction stack.** Diagnostic: a full paragraph with no concrete noun.
7. **Founding-myth inflation.** Diagnostic: the origin story is heroic and unfalsifiable.
8. **Uniform rhythm.** Diagnostic: every paragraph the same length.
9. **Clean resolution.** Diagnostic: no cost, no trade-off, no edge acknowledged.
10. **Untellable story.** Diagnostic: a reader could not retell it accurately after one read.

## Worked example

**Before** (About page opening)

> Founded in 2018, we are a passionate team of innovators dedicated to
> transforming the way modern businesses approach customer engagement. Our
> mission is to empower organisations with cutting-edge solutions that drive
> meaningful results.

Diagnosis: four inflated words in two sentences; no specific anything; passive
construction hiding who did what; nothing a competitor could not also claim; no
position anyone could oppose.

**After**

> We started in 2018 after watching a client spend eleven months and most of a
> budget on a customer platform nobody in their support team would open.
>
> The software was fine. The problem was that nobody had asked the eight people
> who would use it every day what their morning actually looked like.
>
> So we do that first now, before anything is scoped. It makes our projects
> slower to start and considerably harder to sell.

What changed: a specific origin with a real number replaces the founding myth;
the position is stated as a practice rather than a value; the last line names
the cost, which is what makes the rest believable. A competitor could not paste
their name onto it.

## Evidence notes

This reference is practitioner craft. It is not derived from controlled studies,
and long-form brand copy is not an area with a strong empirical literature.
Treat it as well-tested heuristic.

Deliberately omitted: claims about optimal word counts, attention-span
statistics, and the "people don't read anymore" genre. The circulating numbers
have no traceable primary source, and the evidence on reading behaviour is
context-dependent enough that a general rule would be misleading.
